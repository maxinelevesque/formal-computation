Atoms and Probes

Computation is what a type forgets.

A typed program can be given two accounts. A typing derivation says how the program was put together, and a semantic type, which is a predicate on terms, says what the program does when it is used. A good part of the theory of programming languages consists of showing that the two accounts agree.For the syntactic method see A. K. Wright and M. Felleisen, ‘A syntactic approach to type soundness’, Information and Computation 115(1), 1994, 38–94. For types as predicates see R. Milner, ‘A theory of type polymorphism in programming’, Journal of Computer and System Sciences 17(3), 1978, 348–375, and, for a recent use at scale, R. Jung, J.-H. Jourdan, R. Krebbers and D. Dreyer, ‘RustBelt: securing the foundations of the Rust programming language’, Proceedings of the ACM on Programming Languages 2(POPL), 2018, article 66.

This essay explains a framework, still in draft, that draws the same line one level lower, through terms themselves. It separates the atoms from which a term is written from the probes by which a term is tested. In full these are description atoms and behaviour probes. The distinction is easy to miss, because a probe is itself written with atoms, and so the two never look different on the page. Once they are separated, a third thing can be seen between them, which is computation.

The distinction bears on decisions that a language designer makes in any case. It settles which equalities a type checker can decide by running terms and which have to be proved. It explains why an operator that inspects code destroys abstraction. It says what a type is unable to record, which includes the cost of a program. And it shows the usual notion of computation as one setting among several.

The essay goes in this order. It first recalls the two ways of giving a type, by rules and by tests. It defines atoms and probes for a small calculus, with a figure in which probes can be tried on terms. It then draws the running of terms, and shows that one picture of running supports three different equalities. A type is defined as a predicate that running cannot change. The framework’s definition follows, in the vocabulary of operational semantics. Two further sections say what a type cannot see, and what happens when a probe can read code. The section before the summary changes the ingredients one at a time, and shows where the usual notion of computation, that of Turing machines, sits among the results.

Each ingredient keeps one colour through all the figures. Ochre is for descriptions: atoms and the text made from them. Green is for steps and runs. Blue is for observation: probes, what they read, and evidence. Black is for what is kept, which is the line and its points. Each point stands for a group of terms that are counted as equal, and the line sets the groups side by side. Red marks a leak or a clash, and a filled green button sets something running.

The figures are drawn in combinatory logic. They can be redrawn in tree calculus or in interaction nets. The text stays with combinatory logic, and a note under each figure says what changes.

Tree calculus has one constant, the leaf △, and five rules: △ △ y z → y (the K-rule), △ (△ x) y z → x z (y z) (the S-rule), and three rules of triage, which look at the shape of the third argument: △ (△ w x) y △ → w, △ (△ w x) y (△ u) → x u and △ (△ w x) y (△ u v) → y u v. Every tree has one of three shapes: a leaf, a stem △ u or a fork △ u v. A tree with no redex anywhere, inside u and v included, is a normal form. Tree reduction is confluent.lean The figures write K = △△, I = △(△K)△, S x y = △(△x)y and ω = S I I, so that the terms read as in combinatory logic.

Interaction nets. A term is compiled to a net of agents by the eager table of tree-semantics, which has fifteen rules: agents for the leaf, the stem and the fork, and agents for applying, triage, copying and erasing.lean For closed trees, tree-semantics proves the compilation sound: if a tree applied to another evaluates strictly to a value, the compiled net finishes and reads back that value.lean A step is one interaction, between two agents joined at their principal ports. The table is eager, in that a net runs an argument even when that argument will be discarded. In the figure of running, under ‘One step’, the thick green wire joins the two agents about to interact, and the label ‘result’ marks the result port. A net is named by the term read back at its result port, and a variable that a probe supplies is a free wire. Compiling open terms, with free wires for their variables, and reading them back are conventions of the browser’s model, which the proofs do not cover.

Two accounts of a typed program

A type can be given by rules for building a term, or by tests applied to a finished one. The rest of the essay uses the two judgments below and the set ⟦A⟧. How a derivation is built matters less here, and readers who do not know typing judgments can pass over those details.

Syntactic typing

Γ ⊢ t : A

Read: under the assumptions Γ, the term t has type A. The statement is established by a derivation built from rules, one rule for each way of forming a term, and one reasons about it by induction on the derivation.

Semantic typing

t ∈ ⟦A⟧

Read: t belongs to the set of terms that pass the tests for A. The set is a predicate on terms. A term belongs to ⟦A → B⟧ if applying it to any member of ⟦A⟧ yields a member of ⟦B⟧.

The two are connected by the fundamental lemma of logical relations, which states that every typing rule preserves membership in the semantic type. A term that can be derived therefore passes every test that its type imposes.W. W. Tait, ‘Intensional interpretations of functionals of finite type I’, Journal of Symbolic Logic 32(2), 1967, 198–212; G. D. Plotkin, ‘Lambda-definability and logical relations’, Memorandum SAI-RM-4, University of Edinburgh, 1973; R. Statman, ‘Logical relations and the typed λ-calculus’, Information and Control 65(2–3), 1985, 85–97.

The framework applies the same division to terms. A term considered as a piece of text is called a description. It is the tree obtained by grouping the term’s symbols, as a parser does, and two descriptions are equal only if they are the same tree. Descriptions are made of atoms. What a term does when it is used is a separate matter, and it is found out by probes. The next section defines both words.

Atoms and probes

The examples are written in combinatory logic, a calculus with three constants and no binding of variables.M. Schönfinkel, ‘Über die Bausteine der mathematischen Logik’, Mathematische Annalen 92, 1924, 305–316; H. B. Curry and R. Feys, Combinatory Logic, vol. I, North-Holland, 1958. Two terms written side by side form an application of the first to the second, and applications group to the left, so that S K K a means ((S K) K) a. The constants are defined by three rules: I x → x, K x y → x and S x y z → x z (y z). In a functional language I is the identity function and K is const, and S passes one argument to two functions and applies the first result to the second. A term is run by using the rules until none applies. In the rules, x, y and z stand for any terms. In the terms themselves the letters a, b, x and y are variables, which have no rules of their own, and ω abbreviates S I I.

An atom is a node of syntax. Here the atoms are the constants, the variables, and application. A term is a tree of atoms and nothing else, and to ask about its atoms is to ask what the text consists of. A programmer would call this the white-box view.

A probe is an experiment performed on a term, and it is the black-box view. The probe supplies a context with a hole, such as [ ] x y, whose variables are fresh, new names that occur nowhere in the term under test. The term is put in the hole and the result is run. The probe then reads the outcome, under one restriction: it may read only what it supplied. If the outcome is written entirely in the probe’s own variables, as x y is, the probe reads it. If the outcome still contains S, K or I, those symbols are part of the term’s text, and the probe may not look at them. It can only supply another argument and try again. A variable that belongs to the term is treated in the same way. Under [ ] x, the term I gives x, which the probe reads. The term K a gives a, which contains no constant, but the probe may not read it, because a came from the term. When the outcome cannot be read, the figure says ‘not read’, and a run that never reaches a normal form gives no reading either. The six terms of the figure have no variables of their own, so every variable in their outcomes came from the probe. The Lean statements use a plainer rule, under which a probe reads any outcome written in variables alone. For terms with no variables of their own, such as these six, the two rules agree. The stricter rule for terms with variables of their own has not been mechanized. This restriction is a decision about observation. It does not follow from the three rules, and it can be changed while they stay as they are.The definition follows J. H. Morris, Lambda-Calculus Models of Programming Languages, PhD thesis, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, 1968, in which two terms are equivalent if no context distinguishes them. Morris observes only whether a run ends. Reading back the probe’s own variables is a variant suited to a calculus with free variables.

Two terms are the same under every probe if no context, however elaborate and however many times it uses the term, leads to different readings. The figure tries three simple contexts on six terms. For these six the three contexts are enough: terms that agree in the figure agree under every probe.lean

The figure shows two groups of rows that agree. The three identities, I, S K K and S K S, each return their argument, since I x, S K K x and S K S x all run to x. However a context copies or discards such a term, each copy that is applied returns what it is given, whichever identity it is. A copy left unapplied in the outcome puts a constant there, and then the probe may not read the outcome at all. So the three give the same reading in every context. K I and K (S K K) agree for the same reason, since each discards its first argument and leaves an identity.

Application is the plainest case of a symbol with two roles. Read as text, S K K x is a tree with an application node at its root, and that node is an atom like any other. Read as an experiment, the same expression is the probe [ ] x used on S K K. The syntax is the same in both readings. What differs is the question asked of it: in the first reading what the text consists of, and in the second what S K K does.

The checkbox lifts the restriction. With it ticked, a probe runs as before and may then read the whole normal form it reaches, constants included, and the six rows separate.

Type theory already has names for the atoms that do each job. An introduction form is an atom that builds a value of a type, and an elimination form is an atom that builds a probe for one. A pair is built by pairing and tested by asking for either component. A function is built by abstraction, written λ, and tested by applying it to an argument, so application is an atom of the text and also the way a probe uses a term.In a functional language the constructors and λ are introduction forms, and application, projection and pattern matching are elimination forms. A positive type, such as a sum or the natural numbers, is determined by its introduction forms. A negative type, such as a function or a record, is determined by its eliminations. J.-M. Andreoli, ‘Logic programming with focusing proofs in linear logic’, Journal of Logic and Computation 2(3), 1992, 297–347; P. B. Levy, Call-by-Push-Value, Springer, 2003; N. Zeilberger, The Logical Basis of Evaluation Order and Pattern-Matching, PhD thesis, Carnegie Mellon University, 2009. For a language in which negative types are defined by their observations see A. Abel, B. Pientka, D. Thibodeau and A. Setzer, ‘Copatterns: programming infinite structures by observations’, POPL 2013, 27–38.

An atom, then, is a part, and a probe is an experiment. The experiment is assembled from parts, which is why the two are confused.

Running a term

Between writing a term and probing it comes running it. A part of a term that matches the left side of a rule is a redex. A step replaces one redex by the right side of its rule, and a run is a sequence of steps. The framework’s word for a run is computation. A term with no redex is a normal form, and when a run ends in a normal form, that normal form is a value of the term the run began with.Small-step and big-step presentations are due to G. D. Plotkin, ‘A structural approach to operational semantics’, Report DAIMI FN-19, Aarhus University, 1981, and G. Kahn, ‘Natural semantics’, in STACS 87, Lecture Notes in Computer Science 247, Springer, 1987, 22–39.

A term can have more than one redex, and so more than one run. The term K a (I b) has two runs that go all the way to a normal form. The K-rule can be used at once, which discards I b and gives a in one step. Or I b can be run first, which gives K a b and then a, in two steps. The term a is a normal form, because a variable has no rule, and so it is the value of both runs. In combinatory logic any two finite runs from the same term can be continued until they meet, a property called confluence.leanG. Huet and J.-J. Lévy, ‘Computations in orthogonal rewriting systems, I and II’, in J.-L. Lassez and G. Plotkin (eds), Computational Logic: Essays in Honor of Alan Robinson, MIT Press, 1991, 395–443. A term therefore has at most one value, though its runs may differ in length.

The figure shows running from four distances. Closest, there is one term and the step it is about to take. One stage back, the whole run of that term is shown. Further back, fourteen terms are shown with every step between them. In the last view the terms and steps are faded and only the values remain. The term and the order of evaluation can be changed in the figure.

The last view is the classical account of what a program means. A program denotes a value, and a type sorts programs by their values.D. S. Scott and C. Strachey, ‘Toward a mathematical semantics for computer languages’, Technical Monograph PRG-6, Oxford University Computing Laboratory, 1971. Nothing in that account is false. It is obtained from the fuller picture by discarding everything except the line and its points.

The framework takes the whole picture as its object of study. The terms that are joined to one another by steps, taken forwards or backwards, form a fibre. The figure shows terms from five fibres. Four of them contain a normal form, which is the value of every term in the fibre and the term to which each of them can run. The fifth fibre has no normal form. The figure shows three of its terms, the loop ω ω, which run to one another in a cycle.The fourteen terms were chosen by hand, and every arrow is computed. An arrow is drawn for each step whose result is among the fourteen. The three ω ω terms have further steps that lead outside the figure, and their run is always shown arguments-first, because that order returns to its start.

Three equalities

The figure of fourteen terms has three kinds of mark: boxes for descriptions, arrows for steps, and a line of points underneath. The boxes and arrows are fixed by the calculus. Once the atoms and the rules are chosen, the descriptions and the steps are determined. The line is another matter. It records which descriptions are to count as the same, and that question has three reasonable answers.

The three are named here by what decides them, and each name keeps its colour for the rest of the essay. Sameness of text identifies two descriptions only when they are the same tree. It is the equality that atoms give. Sameness after running identifies descriptions that lie in one fibre, that is, descriptions joined to one another by steps. It is the only one of the three defined by running. A proof assistant in which every term it compares has a value can decide this equality by running both sides to their values, and it calls the equality definitional. Sameness under probes identifies descriptions that no probe can tell apart. It is the equality that probes give, and it is usually called contextual or observational equivalence.Morris, cited in note 4, and G. D. Plotkin, ‘LCF considered as a programming language’, Theoretical Computer Science 5(3), 1977, 223–255.

Sameness after running implies sameness under probes: two terms joined by steps cannot be told apart by any probe.lean The reason is that a step inside a term is still a step when the term sits in a context. So if t steps to u, a probe’s run on t can begin with that step and continue as its run on u. By confluence, the property that two runs from one term can always be continued until they meet, the two runs end alike, and the probe reads the same thing from both.

The converse fails, and the terms I, S K K and S K S show it. In the table of six terms and three probes, in the section on atoms and probes, the three had the same readings in every column, so sameness under probes puts them on one point. Each is also a normal form already, and by confluence two different normal forms never lie in one fibre, so sameness after running keeps them apart.lean

Where sameness under probes holds and running does not show it, the equality has to be proved. With addition defined by recursion on its first argument, 0 + x steps to x, but x + 0 does not step at all, because the recursion is waiting on the variable x. The two sides of x + 0 = x agree under every probe, yet no step joins them. In a proof assistant such an equation is a statement to be proved, here by induction on x, and an equality that needs a proof is called propositional. A compiler’s optimiser works with sameness under probes from the other side. It may replace a term by any other over the same point, and it looks for one with a shorter run.

A rule can be added to bring sameness after running closer to sameness under probes. In combinatory logic, tick the box in the figure. The rule S K y → I is sound: for every y, S K y and I agree under every probe.lean Applied to an argument, S K y x runs to x in two steps, whatever y is.lean With the rule added, S K K and S K S step to I, and their three separate points become one, as they were under probes. Something that only a probe could find out is now found by running. The argument given for the three identities in the section on atoms and probes applies to S K y as it does to them. In tree calculus the same rule is unsound, as the note under the figure explains.

The η-rule of the lambda calculus is a rule of this kind. In the lambda calculus λx. t is the function that takes an argument x and returns t, and combinatory logic is the lambda calculus with these bound variables compiled away. The η-rule makes a function f interchangeable with λx. f x, the function that takes a fresh x and returns f applied to x. It says that a function is no more than what it returns when applied, which is the black-box view of the section on atoms and probes.H. P. Barendregt, The Lambda Calculus: Its Syntax and Semantics, revised edition, North-Holland, 1984, treats η and extensionality. For η as an expansion see C. B. Jay and N. Ghani, ‘The virtues of eta-expansion’, Journal of Functional Programming 5(2), 1995, 135–154. When the calculus gives the terms of a type such a rule, a checker decides by running what would otherwise need a proof. Function and record types, which are known by their probes, usually have one. Sums and the natural numbers have laws of the same kind, but those laws are hard to use as steps, and most checkers leave them to be proved.T. Altenkirch, P. Dybjer, M. Hofmann and P. Scott, ‘Normalization by evaluation for typed lambda calculus with coproducts’, LICS 2001, 303–310, shows how much machinery the η-law for sums requires.

Types

Any property of terms is a predicate on descriptions, and it can be drawn as shading on the boxes of the figure of fourteen terms. The property of mentioning K is such a predicate. It would make a poor type, because K a (K a) has the property and the term a, to which it steps, does not. A program would belong to the type before a step and not after it. A predicate of this kind will be said to leak.

In this essay a type is a predicate that does not leak: no step leads from a term that satisfies it to a term that does not, in either direction. A fibre, as before, is a set of terms joined to one another by steps, and a predicate that does not leak shades each fibre entirely or not at all.lean It can therefore be stated by naming points on the line, when the line is drawn for sameness after running and has one point for each fibre. The framework’s word is behaviour, and it is slightly wider: a behaviour may attach evidence to a term, and not only a yes or a no, and a predicate is a behaviour exactly when it does not leak.lean Until the section on changing the ingredients the two coincide.

Every account of types as predicates has a condition of this sort, and the conditions differ. Some require every run of a member to end. The term K a (ω ω), where ω ω is the loop of the section on running, can step straight to a or run its argument for ever, so such a condition excludes it while including a, which is one step away. The condition used here is closure under steps in both directions. It does the part of their work that the fundamental lemma below needs.Tait’s saturated sets are closed under head expansion. Girard’s reducibility candidates are closed under reduction, contain only terms all of whose runs end, and contain a neutral term, one that is not an abstraction, whenever they contain all its one-step reducts. Biorthogonal sets are closed, by their construction, under the tests that define them. The condition used here is simpler to state than these and is not the same as any of them. Tait, cited in note 2; J.-Y. Girard, Y. Lafont and P. Taylor, Proofs and Types, Cambridge University Press, 1989, chapters 6 and 14; A. M. Pitts, ‘Parametric polymorphism and operational equivalence’, Mathematical Structures in Computer Science 10(3), 2000, 321–359; J. Vouillon and P.-A. Melliès, ‘Semantic types: a fresh look at the ideal model for types’, POPL 2004, 52–63.

The control marked ‘Close it’ replaces a leaking predicate by the smallest type that contains it: the result is a type,lean it contains the predicate,lean and it lies inside every type that does.lean It extends the shading across every step it meets, in both directions, until nothing more can be added.

Two predicates in the list should be compared. ‘Runs to I’ is a type, since it shades exactly one fibre.lean No probe defines it, however, because it holds of I and not of S K K, and those two give the same readings under every probe.lean ‘Returns a when applied to a’ is defined by a probe, and its shading is drawn in blue. It is also a type,lean and it shades the fibres of I, S K K and S K S together, which are the fibres that sameness under probes merged. Steps settle which predicates are types. Probes are the usual way of defining one, and a type defined by a probe cannot separate terms that probes cannot separate.lean

The fundamental lemma of the first section said that every rule for building a term preserves membership in the semantic type. It can now be followed for a single atom. Let ⟦A⟧ and ⟦B⟧ be any two types in the sense of this section, that is, predicates that do not leak. An application test for A → B → A takes a term u in ⟦A⟧ and a term v in ⟦B⟧, puts the term under test in the probe [ ] u v, and asks whether the result is in ⟦A⟧. It plugs a term into a context as the probes of the table did, but its verdict is membership in a type and not a reading. A term t is in ⟦A → B → A⟧ if it passes every such test, that is, if t u v is in ⟦A⟧ whenever u is in ⟦A⟧ and v is in ⟦B⟧. The claim is that K satisfies it.lean Take such a u and v. The term K u v steps to u, and u is in ⟦A⟧ by assumption. Since ⟦A⟧ does not leak, K u v is in ⟦A⟧ as well, and that is what the test required.

The argument uses each notion once. K is an atom, [ ] u v is the probe of the test, and K u v → u is a step. The test needs a fact about the term under test, K u v. The fact available is about u, and it is the step that carries it from one to the other. This is the sense in which computation lies between atoms and probes.

The definition

The framework’s definition can now be given in familiar words. A presentation has three parts. The first is a collection of descriptions, generated by atoms. The second is a set of steps between them. The third is an observation. In general an observation pairs the descriptions with a collection of observers and gives each pair a verdict. A probe is one kind of observer, and the observation then says what probes there are and what kind of answer a probe returns. In this essay the probes have been contexts written in the calculus itself, and the answer has been a reading or none. The application tests of the section on types use the same contexts with a different verdict, membership in a type. That is one choice among several, and two later sections change it. One statement in the definition is more exact than the table. A family gives each term a set of possible evidence, such as proofs that the term belongs to a type, and each step a map that carries evidence for the step’s start to evidence for its end. Carrying evidence along a run applies the maps of its steps in turn. A predicate is the family whose set has one element at a term that satisfies it and none elsewhere. In a behaviour the map of each step is invertible, so evidence can be carried across a step in exactly one way, in either direction.lean Subject reduction says that evidence can be carried forward across a step, and subject expansion that it can be carried back. A behaviour also asks that each step carry it in only one way.In the language of category theory: take the category whose objects are descriptions and whose maps are runs. A family is a functor from it to sets. A step s → t gives a map of representable families Hom(t, −) → Hom(s, −), and these maps generate an orthogonal factorization system on the category of families. A family is a behaviour exactly when its map to the terminal family has unique lifting against these maps,lean which is the condition in the text, and exactly when it sends every step to a bijection.lean That the factorization system exists follows from the cited results, since the category of runs is small; it has not been mechanized. The points of the line, when it is drawn for sameness after running, are the connected components of the category. Closing a predicate is a reflector in these terms. Putting another category in the place of sets changes the kind of answer a probe returns. See P. Freyd and G. M. Kelly, ‘Categories of continuous functors, I’, Journal of Pure and Applied Algebra 2(3), 1972, 169–191, and C. Cassidy, M. Hébert and G. M. The statement is about one step at a time. It leaves open whether two different runs between the same two terms carry evidence to the same place. For a predicate the question cannot arise, since a term either satisfies a predicate or does not, and there is nothing further to differ. For evidence with more content it can arise, and the next section shows where.

What a type cannot see

A type was defined as a predicate that no step changes. It shades whole fibres, where a fibre is a set of terms joined by steps, and so it amounts to a set of points on the line. Whether a term belongs to it depends only on the term’s fibre, and not on anything inside the fibre. Three things about the inside matter to a designer: how long the runs are, whether two runs between the same terms are the same run, and whether the order of evaluation decides if a value is reached at all.

Length was seen in the figure of fourteen terms, where K a (I b) reached a in one step or in two. The cost of a run is the number of its steps. That this number is a fair measure of cost, within a polynomial factor of time on a machine, is itself a theorem, and it holds for particular orders of evaluation.P. van Emde Boas, ‘Machine models and simulations’, in J. van Leeuwen (ed.), Handbook of Theoretical Computer Science, vol. A, Elsevier, 1990, 1–66; B. Accattoli and U. Dal Lago, ‘(Leftmost-outermost) beta reduction is invariant, indeed’, Logical Methods in Computer Science 12(1:4), 2016. Whether two runs between the same terms are the same run is not settled by the steps. A calculus may declare that two such runs count as one, and then every behaviour has to respect the declaration, so that evidence carried along either run arrives in the same state. For a predicate it must. For richer evidence it need not, unless the two runs are counted as one. In combinatory logic, the panel of the loop with exits shows a fibre in which every term has the value a, but only some orders of evaluation find it. An order that begins with the outermost redex, that is, the outermost part that matches a rule, does. An order that runs arguments first does not. The loop ω ω by itself, where ω is short for S I I, is a third case. It is the cycle of three terms at the edge of the figure of fourteen terms. It has no exit, and no order of evaluation gives it a value.

When probes can read atoms

The probes so far could read only what they supplied: an outcome counted as read only if it was written in the probe’s own variables. Some calculi give a probe more. In tree calculus a program can ask of any normal form whether it is a leaf, a stem or a fork, and take it apart accordingly, so that a probe can read atoms.B. In such a calculus programs are data. Two normal forms are equal under probes only when they are the same tree, so there is a single equality for values and it is decided by comparing text. What is given up is abstraction. Any change to the normal form of a function can be detected by some program, and so no two different normal forms can be exchanged for one another.

The lambda calculus is at the opposite setting. There a probe tests a function only by applying it to arguments and reading what it returns, as the probes of the table of six terms did with I, S K K and S K S, and sameness under probes is much coarser than sameness after running. The framework does not choose between the two. The observation is one of its three parts, and the checkbox changes it while the descriptions and the steps stay as they are. Tree calculus reaches the same place by another road, by adding steps that let its own contexts do the reading.

Changing the ingredients

A presentation, which is the framework’s name for a calculus as given by its definition, has three ingredients: the descriptions, the steps and the observation. To these must be added one decision, which is how much of the resulting picture to keep. The calculus of this essay made its choices early and then left them alone. Its steps run one way. Its probes supply an argument and read back an answer. And the essay has mostly kept the line, on which each fibre shows as a single point. The definition given above requires none of these choices.

Each way of making the choices will be called a notion of computation. (The word computation by itself still means a run.) The usual notion, that of Turing machines and the lambda calculus, is one of them. Its descriptions are finite. A Turing machine’s program is a finite table of moves, and a description is a configuration of that program, a tape with a head and a state. A step is one move. The steps are given by finitely many rules that run one way, and each rule is local, in that it inspects a bounded part of the description. A probe supplies a number, which fixes the starting configuration, and reads a number from the configuration where the run ends, or reads nothing if the run does not end. And only the line is kept. Its points group programs by the partial function from numbers to numbers that they compute, so that a program is identified with that function.

At this setting something remarkable was found. The calculi proposed independently in the 1930s by Church, Kleene and Turing were proved to put the same points on the line. These points are the partial recursive functions, that is, the functions from numbers to numbers that some Turing machine computes, undefined where the machine does not halt. That much is a theorem about the calculi named. The Church–Turing thesis is a further claim, about every effective method of calculation, that none computes more than these. No calculus with finite descriptions and finitely many local rules has since been found to add a point. A calculus of this kind that reaches all of these points is called universal, and by the theorem for the calculi named, and by the thesis for the rest, every universal calculus has exactly these points on its line. This agreement is the reason the usual notion is commonly taken to be what the word computation means.A. Church, ‘An unsolvable problem of elementary number theory’, American Journal of Mathematics 58(2), 1936, 345–363; S. C. Kleene, ‘λ-definability and recursiveness’, Duke Mathematical Journal 2, 1936, 340–353; A. M. Turing, ‘On computable numbers, with an application to the Entscheidungsproblem’, Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society s2-42(1), 1937, 230–265, and ‘Computability and λ-definability’, Journal of Symbolic Logic 2(4), 1937, 153–163.

The agreement holds at one setting, and two things about it should be noticed. First, the calculi that agree on the line differ in their fibres, that is, in what a run costs and in which runs count as the same. Second, the comparison itself needs an observation that belongs to none of the calculi compared. A Turing machine and a term of combinatory logic have no atoms and no steps in common. A number has to be written down in each of them, as marks on a tape or as a term, and to say that the two compute the same function is to probe both from outside, by way of those writings. The observation is therefore an ingredient in its own right, and the thesis is a statement about one choice of it.That a comparison of calculi depends on how data are written in each is argued in U. Two of the cards are drawn larger here, because they are the furthest from the calculus of this essay. The first is the card on watching a run. A trace is the sequence of actions that a run shows. Two processes are bisimilar if each can match every move of the other and remain matched afterwards. The two processes in the figure have the same traces, a b and a c. They are not bisimilar, because the first can do a and still offer both b and c, and the second cannot. A probe that sees only finished traces finds them equal. The second is the card on groups. Take two generators, a and b, and one relation, which says that ab and ba are the same. A word is then a path on a grid, in which a is a move to the right and b a move up. A step exchanges an adjacent ab for ba, or the reverse, and it moves the path across one square. In place of a value a word has the place where its path ends, which no step changes. An observer that counts the as and the bs reads that place. The fibre over it is the set of all paths to it.

Nothing in this picture resembles a machine. The steps go both ways, so they single out no normal form, and there is no input, no output and no order of evaluation. The three ingredients are present all the same, with the observer that counts letters as the observation. Cost is present too. The number of steps a run has taken is the cost of that run, and the least cost of a run between two words is the area between their paths. With other relations the same questions become hard. Whether two words name one element is called the word problem of the group, and for some groups no procedure decides it.

The last card, on keeping the fibres, needs explaining, because it changes what is kept and not what is given. The picture of running can be kept at three levels. At the first, each fibre is counted as one piece, and the pieces are the points of the line. At the second, the arrowheads are forgotten and a fibre is a graph. A loop in it is a round trip from a term back to itself, such as the pentagon of bracketings read by going down one route and back up the other. Some round trips shrink to nothing. A step followed by the same step taken back cancels, and an agreed equation between two runs lets either replace the other inside a round trip. With the equation that counts the pentagon’s two routes as one, its round trip becomes one route out and the same route back, which cancels. A hole is a round trip that cannot be shrunk to nothing in these ways, and without that equation the pentagon is one. (A hole in this sense is not the hole of a probe’s context.) At the third, the arrowheads are kept, and a normal form is the term at which the runs of a fibre end. Cost can be added to the graph or to the picture with direction, as the length of a route.For length as a structure of the same kind as truth, see F. W. Where evidence depends on the route, a result is no longer a property of a value, or of any one description. It is a property of the way in which one description was reached from another.

The usual notion of computation is thus one place among several, and a distinguished one, since it is where one line serves every universal calculus. It owes its position to three choices: rules that are finite and local, probes that deal in numbers, and the decision to keep only the line. It ceases to be the only notion when any of the three is changed. The framework is an attempt to name the ingredients, so that the other notions can be described in the same terms as this one.

Summary

Atoms are the parts from which a term is written. Probes are experiments on a term, and in this essay they read only what they supplied. Running lies between the two, and its steps join descriptions into fibres. Equality of terms can be taken as sameness of text, as membership of one fibre, or as agreement under every probe, and each of these is coarser than the one before.

A type is a predicate that no step changes. Probes give one way to define a type. In the fundamental lemma for K, since K u v steps to u, membership of u carries back to K u v, the term the application test asks about. Membership in a type depends only on a term’s fibre. A type does not record the length of a run, the difference between two runs, or a loop, whether or not some order of evaluation escapes it.

A presentation has three ingredients: the descriptions, the steps and the observation. The usual notion of computation is one way of choosing them, with finite and local rules that run one way, probes that deal in numbers, and only the line kept. At that setting every universal calculus, meaning one that computes all that a Turing machine can, has the same line, which is why it has been taken for the whole subject. Change what a probe returns or may do, let the steps run both ways, or keep the fibres, and other notions appear. Some of them have results that depend on the route taken, and those results are recorded by no value.Three works stand close to this one. B. P. Hilken, ‘Towards a proof theory of rewriting: the simply typed 2λ-calculus’, Theoretical Computer Science 170(1–2), 1996, 407–444, studies the equations that hold between runs of λ-terms. M. Fiore and P. Saville, ‘Coherence and normalisation-by-evaluation for bicategorical cartesian closed structure’, LICS 2020, 425–439, proves a coherence theorem for such runs when they are invertible. J. C. Baez and C. Williams, ‘Enriched Lawvere theories for operational semantics’, EPTCS 323, 2020, 106–135, obtains denotational semantics from operational semantics by a change of enriching category.

It also ran the net of ω ω outermost first, with no net repeated in the first thousand interactions. These are checks of the browser’s model, and none of them is proved.

Not mechanized.

  • Cited: that any two runs to a value count as one (Lévy’s theorem, in Huet and Lévy); that the squares of the development give Lévy’s way of counting; that the steps generate a factorization system on families (Freyd and Kelly); the results reported on the cards on cost, higher types, processes and proofs, apart from the equal length of finishing runs of nets; and the history of the Church–Turing thesis.
  • Argued by hand: the reading rule for terms with variables of their own, under which a probe reads only the variables it supplied; in tree calculus, that the three runs of K a (I b) count as one and that a lap inside K a (ω ω) counts for nothing; that the tree calculus’s own probes give sameness after running on terms with a value, and that a probe defines ‘runs to I’ in tree calculus with variables; that the loop ω ω stays a hole once every step can be taken back; the extension of the counter to terms with K; the area count on the grid of words; and the two processes with the same traces that are not bisimilar.
  • Open: whether the fibre of the net of ω ω has a hole, or a net in it repeats; whether a counter on nets is a behaviour on the fibre of K a (ω ω).
